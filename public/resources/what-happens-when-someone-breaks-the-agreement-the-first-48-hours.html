When Someone Breaks the Agreement

The breach feels like chaos. It's a sequence.

When a client doesn't pay, or walks off mid-project, or you miss a date you promised, the first instinct is panic or anger. Both are expensive. The agreement you signed turns this from a crisis into a process — because you already know the terms, the date, and the consequence. The first 48 hours set the tone for everything after.

The calm sequence

  1. Document, don't accuse. Write down what was agreed, what happened, and the date. The agreement's reference makes this trivial — you have the record.
  2. Notify in writing, once, neutrally. "Per our agreement [ref], delivery was due [date] and payment [amount] is outstanding. Can we resolve this?" No emotion, no threat — just the facts and the paper.
  3. Offer the cure period. Good faith first: a reasonable window to fix it. Most breaches are confusion or cash-flow, not malice, and a fair window resolves them.
  4. Decide from the terms, not the feeling. If the cure lapses, the agreement already says what happens. You're not improvising revenge; you're executing a plan you made when everyone was calm.

Why the paper changes your posture

Without an agreement you negotiate from emotion — hurt, anger, desperation — and you overreact or under-act. With one, you negotiate from record, and the other side feels the difference immediately. A calm message citing a signed reference is far more powerful than an angry call, and it keeps your dignity and your options intact.

The agreement isn't for the day everything goes right. It's for the day it doesn't — so you respond from a plan instead of from pain.
