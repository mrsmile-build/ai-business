How to Deal with Customers Who Owe You Money: Proven Strategies

How to Deal with Customers Who Owe You Money

Dealing with unpaid invoices is one of the most stressful aspects of running a business. While chasing payments can feel like a nuisance, the way you handle the situation can either preserve a valuable relationship or destroy it forever. In this guide, we’ll walk you through practical, step‑by‑step strategies that let you recover the money you’re owed, keep your brand reputation intact, and put safeguards in place so the problem doesn’t repeat.

---

1. Understand the Situation: Legal & Financial Implications

Before you pick up the phone, take a moment to assess the full picture. Not every late payment is a sign of a bad customer—sometimes it’s a cash‑flow glitch, a clerical error, or a misunderstanding about the terms.

1.1 Review the Contract

  • Payment terms – Are the due dates, late‑fee clauses, and acceptable payment methods clearly stated?
  • Scope of work – Verify that the deliverables match what the client received. Discrepancies can be a legitimate reason for withholding payment.
  • Dispute resolution – Many contracts include a mediation or arbitration clause that can save you time and legal fees later.

1.2 Know Your Legal Rights

Each jurisdiction has its own small‑claims thresholds and collection laws. In Nigeria, for example, the Bills of Exchange Act and the Commercial Court provide mechanisms to enforce unpaid invoices. Knowing these options helps you decide whether to proceed informally or involve a lawyer.

1.3 Calculate the Real Cost of Non‑Payment

Beyond the headline amount, consider:

  • Interest and late fees – If your contract permits, add statutory interest (e.g., 10% per annum) to encourage prompt settlement.
  • Administrative overhead – Time spent on reminders, phone calls, and legal counsel adds up.
  • Opportunity cost – Money tied up in delinquent accounts can’t be reinvested in growth.

Understanding these numbers gives you a stronger negotiating position and clarifies whether the debt is worth pursuing.

---

2. Communication Best Practices: From Friendly Reminder to Firm Notice

The tone and timing of your outreach can make the difference between a quick payment and a burnt bridge.

2.1 Start with a Friendly Reminder

  • Timing – Send a polite reminder one to three days after the due date. Use a subject line like "Invoice #12345 – Friendly Reminder".
  • Content – Keep it short, include the invoice number, amount, due date, and a clear call‑to‑action (e.g., "Please settle by Friday to avoid late fees.")
  • Medium – Email is standard, but a quick SMS or WhatsApp message can be more effective for local clients.

2.2 Follow‑Up with a Firm but Respectful Tone

If the first reminder goes unanswered, send a second notice after 5‑7 days:

  • Re‑state the original terms.
  • Mention any accrued interest or late fees.
  • Offer a payment plan or alternative method (e.g., split the amount into two installments).

2.3 The Final Notice: Setting Boundaries

Should the customer still not respond after two reminders, issue a final notice (usually 14‑21 days after the invoice date):

  • Clearly state the consequences – suspension of services, referral to a collection agency, or legal action.
  • Include a deadline – e.g., "Please pay in full by 30 Oct 2026, or we will proceed with formal recovery proceedings."
  • Attach the original invoice and any prior correspondence for reference.

2.4 Keep a Paper Trail

Save every email, call log, and SMS. This documentation is invaluable if you need to involve a lawyer or a collection agency.

3. Structured Payment Plans & Modern Collection Tools

Sometimes a customer genuinely cannot pay the full amount immediately. Offering a structured plan can preserve goodwill and improve cash flow.

3.1 Designing a Viable Payment Plan

1. Assess the client’s capacity – Ask for a brief statement of cash flow or a realistic payment schedule.

2. Set milestones – Break the total into equal installments (e.g., 3 × ₦50,000 over three months).

3. Document the agreement – Update the original contract with an addendum that includes the new dates, amounts, and any interest.

4. Automate reminders – Use calendar invites or task management tools to trigger alerts before each due date.

4. Legal Remedies & When to Escalate

Even with the best communication, some debts become irrecoverable. Knowing when to involve legal mechanisms protects your bottom line.

4.1 When to Involve a Lawyer

  • High‑value invoices – Typically above ₦500,000 (or your local threshold).
  • Repeated non‑payment – The client has a history of late or missed payments.
  • Evidence of fraud – The client disputes the work without merit.

A lawyer can draft a demand letter, which often prompts payment because it signals serious intent.

4.2 Small Claims Court

For amounts under the statutory limit (e.g., ₦2 million in many Nigerian states), you can file a claim without a lawyer. Prepare:

  • Original contract
  • All correspondence
  • Invoices and proof of delivery
  • Proof of loss (interest, fees)

4.3 Engaging a Collection Agency

If the debt is sizable but you lack the resources for a lawsuit, a reputable collection agency can recover the amount minus a commission (usually 10‑25%). Ensure the agency complies with the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (or local equivalent) to avoid reputational damage.

4.4 Reporting to Credit Bureaus

In Nigeria, the Credit Bureau Limited (CBN) allows businesses to report delinquent accounts. A negative credit report can motivate payment while also protecting future vendors.

---

5. Preventive Measures: Turning Debt Recovery into a Competitive Advantage

The best strategy is one that prevents the problem from happening again.

5.1 Clear, Up‑Front Terms

  • Include payment due dates, late‑fee percentages, and acceptable payment methods in every proposal.
  • Highlight these terms in a bold box on the invoice.

5.2 Require Up‑Front Deposits

For new or high‑risk clients, ask for a 30‑50% deposit before starting work. This reduces exposure and shows the client’s commitment.

5.3 Credit Checks for Large Contracts

Use services like **[Namecheap Business Verification](https://www.namecheap.com/ (or local equivalents) to assess creditworthiness before signing a multi‑month agreement.

5.4 Offer Multiple Payment Options

Customers are more likely to pay on time when they can choose a method that suits them—bank transfer, card, mobile money, or Paystack/Flutterwave links.

5.5 Automate Invoicing & Reminders

Set up recurring invoices for subscription‑type services and schedule automatic reminder emails 3 days before the due date, on the due date, and 5 days after.
