The Skill You Already Have That You Don't Count as a Skill

"Anyone can do that" is the loudest clue

Ask someone what they're good at and they'll name the hard, impressive things — the degree, the trade, the title. Then they'll dismiss the real asset with a shrug: "oh, that? anyone can do that." That shrug is the tell. The thing that feels easy to you is easy because you've already solved it — and for the person who hasn't, it's worth money. Fluency hides value from the fluent.

So the first move isn't to acquire a skill. It's to notice the one you've been sitting on.

The three-question audit

Write the first honest answer to each. No filtering, no "but that's not a real business."

  1. What do people ask me for? The favors you keep getting asked for are market signals wearing a friend's face. "Can you fix my phone?", "Can you write this for me?", "Can you help me carry this?" — each is a service someone already values.
  2. What do I do without thinking? The task that bores you is the task others fear. Boredom is the fingerprint of mastery you've stopped noticing.
  3. What did I solve last week that took me five minutes? Five minutes for you can be a week of stress for someone else. That gap is your price.

Turn the answer into a one-line offer

You don't need a brand, a website, or a business plan. You need a sentence a stranger understands in three seconds: "I help [who] [get what result] without [the pain]." Example: "I help small shops set up their WhatsApp so customers stop getting lost." That's a business. The rest is packaging you add once someone pays.

And here's the part most people miss: you can test the offer before you build anything. Say the sentence to five people. If three tilt their heads and ask "how much?", you have a business. If none do, you've lost five minutes and learned something real. That's cheaper than any plan.
