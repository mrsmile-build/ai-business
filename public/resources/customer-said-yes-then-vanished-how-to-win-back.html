They Said Yes, Then Vanished — How to Win Them Back

The post-yes ghost is the most painful kind

Losing a lead who never replied is one thing. Losing someone who said yes — who shook on it, paid a deposit, sent the details — and then went quiet is a different wound. It hurts because it felt real. And it is more common than you think: a "yes" is not a signature, it is a mood, and moods move.

This is not the same as following up before a decision (that guide is here). This is the follow-up after the yes, when the work hasn't started and the silence has.

---

Why they vanished (and it's rarely you)

  • Life happened. A bill, a family issue, a boss who said no. The yes was honest; the circumstances changed.
  • The excitement faded. Saying yes felt good in the moment; doing it now feels like work, and they're avoiding the friction of starting.
  • They got cold feet about the money. The decision was emotional; the invoice was real.
  • Someone else offered them a way out. A cheaper option let them save face by "thinking about it" instead of admitting they wobbled.

Notice: three of the four are not about your quality. So the first job is to remove friction, not to re-pitch.

---

The win-back sequence (three touches, ten days)

WhenMessageGoal
Day 1 of silence"Hey [name], just confirming we're still good for [the thing] — I've held the slot/started the prep. Anything changed on your side?"Make staying easy; give them a graceful way to say life got in the way
Day 4One specific, useful nudge: a photo, a small progress step you did anyway, a question only they can answer. No pressure.Remind them the work is real and you're already in motion
Day 10"No stress at all if the timing's off — I'll release the slot so you're not locked in. If it's still a yes, just say go and we start this week."Take the obligation off; a released slot often triggers the real yes

The last message is the strongest, because it removes the guilt that's keeping them silent. People reply to an exit far faster than to a demand.
